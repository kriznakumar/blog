Motivation: understanding transformer training instabilities

Transformer training can exhibit oscillating loss, sudden excursions, or divergence. Empirical studies have connected some of these behaviors to the growth of attention logits and to highly concentrated attention distributions (Wortsman et al.; Zhai et al.). Understanding such behavior requires more than detecting an increase in the loss. We need to distinguish an excursion from which training can recover, a persistent oscillation, and a trajectory that continues to grow.

These distinctions matter when interpreting the learning rate. A step can amplify small perturbations near a solution without sending the parameters to infinity. Nonlinear feedback may keep the iterates bounded even after local contraction fails. Conversely, a local curvature calculation cannot establish that such feedback will occur. Studying local contraction, global boundedness, and oscillation amplitude separately tells us what a curvature threshold does and does not imply about the subsequent trajectory. It also explains why a continuous gradient-flow description may omit behavior caused by finite steps.

Mini-batch training introduces another distinction. Even when the sampled gradient has the correct conditional mean, the realized update depends on which examples are selected. The size of typical fluctuations and the probability of an unusually consequential update are different quantities. A variance calculation can describe how averaging more examples changes the noise scale, while an exit probability also depends on the distribution’s tails and the current parameters. Moreover, the expected value of a nonlinear diagnostic need not equal that diagnostic evaluated at the expected update. At a fixed state, increasing batch size under independent sampling concentrates updates around the same expected gradient; changing the learning rate also changes the deterministic step. Distinguishing these effects matters when interpreting either intervention.

Attention involves products of learned transformations, so changing one parameter block can change the gradient experienced by another. In the restricted linear self-attention model studied here, this interaction reduces exactly to two factors whose prediction depends on their product. Equivalent products can have different parameter scales and different local stability. The reduction therefore retains a mechanism through which parameterization, learning rate, and sampled data jointly affect finite updates.

We use that reduction to study the same map throughout the article. It has an explicit invariant region, an exactly solvable two-cycle, and a scalar representation of batch variation. These features let us compare local curvature, discrete dynamics, effective flows, stochastic moments, moving boundaries, and parameterization without changing the underlying optimization problem. Each conclusion can be checked against the same update, rather than compared across unrelated examples.

The purpose is to clarify mechanisms and the evidence needed to distinguish them. Remaining in a bounded region does not establish decreasing loss, and leaving that region does not establish divergence. The exact reduction also requires a shared invariant plane; it does not describe arbitrary softmax transformers with depth and optimizer state. Within this scope, the following questions separate the deterministic and stochastic aspects of instability that the different analyses can address.

The common map

Consider the normalized loss

\[L_\mu(A,B)=\frac12(AB-\mu)^2,\qquad \mu>0.\]

The prediction is \(AB\). A unit gradient step, with \(e=AB-\mu\), is

\[\Phi_\mu(A,B)=\bigl(A-eB,\ B-eA\bigr).\]

Although the loss is a squared error in the prediction, it is quartic in the parameters. Updating one factor multiplies the error by the other factor. Consequently, the parameter scale changes the size of the next correction. The map retains this feedback even in two dimensions.

The unit step is a normalization, not an assumption that the physical learning rate equals one. To see where the learning rate enters, start with per-example losses

\[\ell_i(a,b)=\frac12(k_iab-y_i)^2.\]

For a fixed training collection of \(n\) examples, define

\[C=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n k_i^2>0,\qquad T=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n k_i y_i>0.\]

Here \(C\) measures the scale of the mode and \(T\) its target alignment. For gradient descent with step size \(\eta>0\), the change of variables

\[A=\sqrt{\eta C}\,a,\qquad B=\sqrt{\eta C}\,b,\qquad \mu=\eta T\]

gives exactly the map above. In particular, increasing \(\eta\) changes both \(\mu\) and the normalized initial point.

Derivation of the normalization and batch update

The average loss has gradients \(\partial_a\ell=(Cab-T)b\) and \(\partial_b\ell=(Cab-T)a\). Thus

\[\begin{aligned}A^+&=\sqrt{\eta C}\,[a-\eta(Cab-T)b]\\&=A-\eta(Cab-T)B\\&=A-(AB-\eta T)B=A-(AB-\mu)B.\end{aligned}\]

The calculation for \(B^+\) is identical with the factors interchanged. For a batch \(\mathsf B\) of size \(m\), set \(C_{\mathsf B}=m^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathsf B}k_i^2\) and \(T_{\mathsf B}=m^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathsf B}k_i y_i\). Its normalized residual multiplier is

\[\eta(C_{\mathsf B}ab-T_{\mathsf B})=\frac{C_{\mathsf B}}{C}AB-\eta T_{\mathsf B}.\]

Hence \(\alpha=C_{\mathsf B}/C\) and \(\nu=\eta T_{\mathsf B}\) give the batch map below. The normalized full loss is \(\eta^2C\) times the original average loss, up to an additive constant; it is not the original loss measured in its original units.

This factorized model also arises in a restricted sector of one-layer linear self-attention. In the shared-mode reduction, each prompt has a quadratic prediction \(u^\top H_i u\), and the symmetric matrices \(H_i\) share two orthonormal eigenvectors with paired eigenvalues. Initialization in their span keeps every prompt gradient in that span. A fixed coordinate change then gives the losses \(\ell_i\) above, with the coordinate change absorbed into the effective step size \(\eta\). Exact sharing and initialization in the invariant plane are substantive assumptions. The analysis below concerns this plane; it does not assume that arbitrary attention training remains two-dimensional.

A mini-batch changes two coefficients:

\[(A^+,B^+)=\bigl(A-(\alpha AB-\nu)B,\ B-(\alpha AB-\nu)A\bigr).\]

The full batch has \((\alpha,\nu)=(1,\mu)\). We assume that batches are sampled freshly and uniformly with replacement, unless stated otherwise, so their conditional expectations at the current state satisfy \(\mathbb E[\alpha\mid A,B]=1\) and \(\mathbb E[\nu\mid A,B]=\mu\). Let

\[p=AB,\qquad \rho=\nu-\mu-(\alpha-1)p.\]

Then, with \(X=(A,B)\), the stochastic update is

\[X^+=\Phi_\mu(X)+\rho(B,A),\qquad \mathbb E[\rho\mid X]=0,\qquad \mathbb E[\rho^2\mid X]=v(p).\]

All batch variation therefore enters through a single scalar perturbation whose distribution depends on the current product. The following sections examine this same update. Whenever the invariant ellipse is used, we additionally assume \(0<\mu<2\).

1. Local curvature: will a perturbation shrink?

Question Q1.

The first question is local. Suppose the parameters nearly fit the target. Does the next full-batch step reduce a small error normal to the solution set? This is the role of the Hessian in the classical step-size condition and in discussions of the edge of stability.

Differentiating \(\nabla L_\mu=e(B,A)\) gives

\[\nabla^2L_\mu(A,B)=\begin{pmatrix}B^2&2AB-\mu\\2AB-\mu&A^2\end{pmatrix}.\]

At a solution, \(AB=\mu\), this becomes \(uu^\top\), where \(u=(B,A)\). It has eigenvalues \(S=A^2+B^2\) and zero: indeed, \(uu^\top u=Su\), while \(uu^\top(A,-B)=0\). The Jacobian of the unit-step map is \(I_2-\nabla^2L_\mu\), so its corresponding multipliers are \(1-S\) and one. Normal perturbations contract in the linearization precisely when

\[|1-S|<1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 0<S<2.\]

The unit multiplier is expected: every point on the hyperbola \(AB=\mu\) is a fixed point. This calculation concerns attraction toward the solution set, not strict contraction toward an isolated minimizer.

At the balanced solution \(A=B=\sqrt\mu\), we have \(S=2\mu\). Its normal multiplier is \(1-2\mu\), which reaches \(-1\) at \(\mu=1\). Thus \(0<\mu<1\) is the local linear contraction regime. The nonlinear behavior at and beyond this threshold requires another calculation.

For mini-batches, one can instead examine curvature along the batch gradient. The batch potential is \(\tfrac12\alpha A^2B^2-\nu AB\), up to a constant, and its gradient is \((\alpha AB-\nu)u\). At \(X\ne0\), the directional curvature is

\[\frac{u^\top\nabla^2L_{\alpha,\nu}u}{u^\top u}=\frac{\alpha(A^4+B^4+4A^2B^2)-2\nu AB}{S}.\]

To obtain the numerator, the diagonal Hessian terms contribute \(\alpha B^4+\alpha A^4\), and the two off-diagonal terms contribute \(2AB(2\alpha AB-\nu)\). Taking the conditional expectation replaces \(\alpha\) by one and \(\nu\) by \(\mu\). Thus the expected directional curvature equals its full-batch counterpart at this fixed state.

This is the expectation-of-quotients convention used for batch sharpness. At a zero-residual batch, the Hessian is \(\alpha uu^\top\), so its largest eigenvalue also equals the displayed quantity. A gradient-magnitude-weighted convention need not have the same mean. Nor does the fixed-state identity imply that runs with different batch sizes visit states with the same curvature.

The local calculation gives a precise threshold, but it leaves open what an unstable perturbation does afterward. It also reveals a limitation of this particular example: every batch gradient is collinear with \(u\). The map retains random gradient magnitudes while removing variation in gradient direction.

2. Discrete dynamics: can the iterates remain bounded?

Questions Q1, Q2, Q3.

Once a solution loses local attraction, convergence is no longer the only relevant question. The iterates may remain in a bounded region, settle into a cycle, or exhibit more complicated motion. These possibilities require retaining the finite update, as in analyses of nonlinear gradient-descent dynamics.

For \(0<\mu<2\), define the signed quadratic coordinate

\[D_\mu(A,B)=A^2+B^2-\mu AB-(4-\mu^2).\]

The full-batch map satisfies the exact identity

\[D_\mu(\Phi_\mu(X))=q_\mu(e)D_\mu(X),\qquad q_\mu(e)=e^2+\mu e+1.\]

Since \(q_\mu(e)=(e+\mu/2)^2+1-\mu^2/4>0\), the sign of \(D_\mu\) is preserved. Moreover,

\[A^2+B^2-\mu AB=\frac{2-\mu}{4}(A+B)^2+\frac{2+\mu}{4}(A-B)^2.\]

Both coefficients are positive. Therefore \(D_\mu\le0\) is a compact region, and every full-batch orbit starting there remains bounded. Its boundary is the ellipse

\[\frac{(A+B)^2}{2+\mu}+\frac{(A-B)^2}{2-\mu}=4.\]

The algebra behind the preservation identity appears in the derivations. This boundedness statement applies throughout \(0<\mu<2\), including values above the balanced solution’s local threshold \(\mu=1\). There is no contradiction: local contraction and bounded motion are different properties.

The same distinction appears at a fixed value of \(\mu\). On the solution set, \(D_\mu=S-4\). Solutions with \(2<S<4\) lie inside the ellipse but expand sufficiently small normal perturbations in the linearization. Solutions with \(S>4\) lie outside and are still fixed points. The ellipse is therefore a boundedness certificate, not a claim that every exterior point diverges.

For example, at \(\mu=1\), both \((1,1)\) and \((2,\tfrac12)\) have zero loss. Their boundary coordinates are \(-2\) and \(1/4\), respectively. Loss alone cannot identify which side of the ellipse contains the parameters. In the interior, however, it supplies a one-way check. With \(w=A-B\),

\[D_\mu=w^2-(2-\mu)(2-e).\]

Thus \(D_\mu<0\) implies \(e<2\). Also \(S\ge2|p|\); if \(p\le\mu-2<0\), then \(D_\mu\ge-(2+\mu)p-(4-\mu^2)\ge0\). Hence the interior also has \(e>-2\), and consequently \(L_\mu<2\). A normalized loss of at least two excludes strict interior membership; a smaller loss does not establish it.

The nonlinear recurrence also describes motion within the region. Multiplying the updated factors gives \(p^+=(1+e^2)p-eS\), while subtracting them gives \(w^+=(1+e)w\). Using \(p=e+\mu\) and \(S=w^2+2p\), we obtain

\[\begin{aligned}e^+&=(1+e^2)(e+\mu)-e\,[w^2+2(e+\mu)]-\mu\\&=e^3+(\mu-2)e^2+(1-2\mu-w^2)e,\\w^+&=(1+e)w.\end{aligned}\]

On the boundary, \(w^2=(2-\mu)(2-e)\), so substitution gives \(e^+=e^3-3e\). For \(e=2\cos\theta\), the identity \(4\cos^3\theta-3\cos\theta=\cos3\theta\) yields \(e^+=2\cos3\theta\). The boundary thus supports the folding of an interval associated with a Chebyshev map. This is an exact statement about boundary motion; it does not assert that a typical interior trajectory is chaotic.

Discrete dynamics supplies the global geometric context missing from a local Hessian calculation. An analysis of the full-batch map alone still omits random switching between batch maps. Studying that switching is a further dynamical question, not a limitation of discrete dynamical systems as a framework.

3. Effective flows: what governs an oscillation’s amplitude?

Question Q3.

Following every iterate is not always the most useful description. Near the first loss of local stability, successive iterates alternate, while their amplitude evolves more slowly. A two-step map can separate these changes. This motivates two-step analyses and, more broadly, continuous descriptions such as central flows and rod flow. Those constructions have their own assumptions; the calculation here is a local two-step expansion.

First consider ordinary gradient flow for the same normalized loss:

\[\dot A=-eB,\qquad\dot B=-eA.\]

Differentiating the product gives

\[\dot e=\dot A B+A\dot B=(-eB)B+A(-eA)=-e(A^2+B^2)=-eS.\]

In particular, \(e(t)=e(0)\exp[-\int_0^tS(s)\,ds]\), so the error preserves its sign and its magnitude cannot increase. This flow cannot represent a finite-step sign alternation. Also, when taking a physical small-step limit, one must remember that the normalization makes \(\mu\) depend on \(\eta\).

Now restrict the discrete map to the invariant line \(A=B\). Its error map is

\[F_\mu(e)=e^3+(\mu-2)e^2+(1-2\mu)e.\]

Write \(\mu=1+\varepsilon\), with \(\varepsilon>0\) small. Expanding the composition near \(e=0\) gives

\[F_\mu(F_\mu(e))-e=4\varepsilon e-4e^3+O\!\left(\varepsilon e^2+\varepsilon^2|e|+|e|^4\right).\]

On the scale \(e=O(\sqrt\varepsilon)\), the two leading terms have comparable size. They predict an oscillation amplitude of order \(\sqrt\varepsilon\). There is an exact benchmark: the positive, nonconstant two-cycle on the balanced line has error values

\[e_\pm=\frac{-\varepsilon\pm\sqrt{\varepsilon^2+4\varepsilon}}{2}.\]

Indeed, \(e_\pm/\sqrt\varepsilon=-\sqrt\varepsilon/2\pm\sqrt{1+\varepsilon/4}\), which tends to \(\pm1\). The local expansion therefore recovers the leading amplitude of the exact cycle. Both calculations are given in the derivations.

The useful conclusion is specific: averaging over two steps identifies the scale of an emerging oscillation. The displayed envelope does not by itself reconstruct every intervening iterate, establish behavior far from \(\mu=1\), or estimate an unusual batch jump. Those questions require additional information from the discrete map.

4. Stochastic moments: what does covariance determine?

Questions Q4, Q5.

For a batch-size comparison, a natural first calculation is the conditional mean and covariance of the update. These quantities describe the scale and direction of fluctuations, and they also enter diffusion approximations. The moment calculation itself does not require an SDE approximation.

The batch gradient is \(g_{\mathsf B}=(e-\rho)u\), where \(u=(B,A)\). Since \(\mathbb E[\rho\mid X]=0\), its centered part is \(-\rho u\), and hence

\[\operatorname{Cov}(g_{\mathsf B}\mid X)=v(p)uu^\top=v(p)\begin{pmatrix}B^2&AB\\AB&A^2\end{pmatrix}.\]

The covariance has rank at most one; it has rank one when \(X\ne0\) and \(v(p)>0\). If \(e\ne0\) and \(X\ne0\), its trace divided by the squared mean-gradient norm is \(v(p)S/(e^2S)=v(p)/e^2\). At a full-batch solution, the mean gradient vanishes and this ratio is not defined.

Under independent sampling with replacement, \(\rho=m^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^m Z_{i_j}(p)\), where

\[Z_i(p)=\eta(k_i y_i-T)-p\left(\frac{k_i^2}{C}-1\right),\qquad\mathbb E[Z_i(p)]=0.\]

Independence makes the cross terms in the squared sum vanish, giving \(v(p)=\operatorname{Var}(Z_i(p))/m\). This is an exact fixed-state batch-size law.

In this model, covariance determines more than an approximate fluctuation scale. Let \(Y=\Phi_\mu(X)\). Expanding the quadratic \(D_\mu(Y+\rho u)\) gives

\[\begin{aligned}D_\mu(Y+\rho u)=D_\mu(Y)&+\rho\,[2Y_1B+2Y_2A-\mu(Y_1A+Y_2B)]\\&+\rho^2(S-\mu p).\end{aligned}\]

The linear term has conditional mean zero. Using the deterministic identity and \(S-\mu p=D_\mu(X)+4-\mu^2\), we obtain

\[\mathbb E[D_\mu(X^+)\mid X]=\bigl(q_\mu(e)+v(p)\bigr)D_\mu(X)+(4-\mu^2)v(p).\]

There are no omitted terms: the observable is quadratic and the displacement is linear in \(\rho\). On \(D_\mu=0\), its conditional mean after one step is \((4-\mu^2)v(p)>0\) whenever the variance is positive. Thus unbiased parameter updates can produce a positive mean boundary coordinate.

This finite-step identity should be distinguished from a continuous-time model. Stochastic modified equations and related SDE approximations additionally require a regime in which their drift and diffusion approximate the discrete process. Knowing the first two moments alone establishes neither that approximation nor a boundary-crossing probability. The latter depends on how much probability lies beyond particular thresholds, which is the next question.

5. Batch-dependent boundaries: which updates leave the ellipse?

Questions Q2, Q5, Q6, Q7.

Suppose the current state lies inside the full-batch ellipse. We now ask whether one realized batch leaves that region. Unlike the mean-drift question, this concerns an event. It is the focus of the moving-boundary analysis for the shared-mode model.

For a fixed batch with \(\alpha>0\), the rescaling \((\widetilde A,\widetilde B)=\sqrt\alpha(A,B)\) turns its update into \(\Phi_\nu\): the batch multiplier \(\alpha AB-\nu\) becomes \(\widetilde A\widetilde B-\nu\). When \(|\nu|<2\), the same quadratic identity therefore gives the batch its own invariant ellipse,

\[\frac{\alpha(A+B)^2}{2+\nu}+\frac{\alpha(A-B)^2}{2-\nu}=4.\]

Its coefficients vary with the sampled batch. A region preserved by each repeated use of one batch need not be preserved by switching among batches. The criterion below compares every sampled update with the fixed full-batch ellipse; it remains valid even when a particular batch has \(|\nu|\ge2\).

For the current state, abbreviate \(D=D_\mu(X)\), and define

\[K=S-\mu p=D+4-\mu^2,\qquad J=(2e+\mu)D+(4-\mu^2)e.\]

The exact one-step formula is

\[D_\mu(X^+)=K\rho^2-J\rho+q_\mu(e)D.\]

For a nonzero interior state, \(K>0\) and \(q_\mu(e)D<0\). The quadratic therefore has two real roots of opposite signs,

\[\rho_\pm(X)=\frac{J\pm\sqrt{J^2-4Kq_\mu(e)D}}{2K},\qquad\rho_-<0<\rho_+.\]

It is negative exactly between the roots. Hence the next iterate remains strictly inside if and only if \(\rho\in(\rho_-,\rho_+)\). It lies strictly outside if and only if \(\rho<\rho_-\) or \(\rho>\rho_+\); equality places it on the boundary. This distinction matters for discrete batch distributions, which can put positive mass at a root. The origin is excluded from the formula because \(K=0\) there; every batch fixes it.

The interval depends on the state as well as on \(\mu\). A variance describes the size of the perturbation distribution, whereas these roots describe the available tolerance in each direction. Both are needed to assess a batch-size intervention.

A concrete example separates the two kinds of information. Take \(\mu=1\) and \(X=(1,1)\). Then \(e=0\), \(X^+=(1+\rho,1+\rho)\), and

\[D_1(X^+)=(1+\rho)^2-3.\]

The strict interior interval is therefore \((-1-\sqrt3,-1+\sqrt3)\). Compare these two centered perturbation laws:

LawValues and probabilitiesVarianceProbability of \(D_1(X^+)>0\)
A\(-\tfrac12,+\tfrac12\), each with probability \(\tfrac12\)\(1/4\)\(0\)
B\(-1,0,+1\), with probabilities \(\tfrac18,\tfrac34,\tfrac18\)\(1/4\)\(1/8\)

Both laws can be realized with \(\alpha=1\) and \(\nu=1+\rho\). Their conditional update means and covariances agree. Under law A, both perturbations remain inside; under law B, only \(\rho=1\) crosses, giving probability \(1/8\). Their expected boundary coordinate is nevertheless the same: \(\mathbb E[(1+\rho)^2-3]=-2+1/4=-7/4\).

Batch averaging can control these tails under an explicit assumption. Suppose the centered single-example fluctuation satisfies

\[\mathbb E\exp(sZ_i(p))\le\exp\!\left(\frac{s^2\kappa(p)^2}{2}\right)\quad\text{for every real }s.\]

For \(\kappa(p)>0\), independent sampling then gives the bound

\[\begin{aligned}\Pr\{D_\mu(X^+)\ge0\mid X\}&\le \exp\!\left(-\frac{m\rho_-^2}{2\kappa(p)^2}\right)+\exp\!\left(-\frac{m\rho_+^2}{2\kappa(p)^2}\right)\\&\le2\exp\!\left(-\frac{m\tau(X)^2}{2\kappa(p)^2}\right),\qquad\tau(X)=\min\{-\rho_-,\rho_+\}.\end{aligned}\]

The proof is given below. If \(\kappa(p)=0\), the moment-generating-function bound forces \(Z_i(p)=0\) almost surely, so an interior state follows the full-batch update and cannot leave in one step. The exponential expression is an upper bound at a fixed state, learning rate, and population. It is not an exact probability law or a guarantee that crossing probabilities decrease monotonically for every integer batch size.

A bound for a whole run must also control how \(\tau(X)/\kappa(p)\) changes along the random trajectory. Moreover, increasing batch size concentrates the sampled coefficients at a fixed learning rate, whereas changing the learning rate alters the map, the normalized state, and its boundary. Matching a noise scale does not make these interventions equivalent.

Finally, leaving the ellipse means losing this full-batch boundedness certificate. It does not prove a loss spike, a catapult phase, or eventual divergence. Those are statements about the subsequent trajectory. The exact roots answer the narrower question of whether a specified batch leaves the reference region.

Containment and progress. Testing that each accepted iterate remains inside the ellipse maintains the same bound \(L_\mu<2\). If the current loss is \(1.9\), the next successful test still certifies only \(L_\mu^+<2\); it does not certify \(L_\mu^+<1.9\). No iteration bound for reaching a prescribed smaller loss follows from that test.

A full-batch step that stays inside while increasing the loss

Take \(\mu=1\) and \(A=B=\sqrt{3/2}\). The error and loss are

\[e=AB-1=\frac12,\qquad L_1(A,B)=\frac12\left(\frac12\right)^2=\frac18.\]

On the balanced line, \(D_1(A,A)=2A^2-A^2-3=A^2-3\), so the starting point satisfies \(D_1=3/2-3=-3/2<0\). The full-batch update is

\[A^+=A-eB=\frac A2,\qquad B^+=B-eA=\frac B2,\qquad A^+B^+=\frac{AB}{4}=\frac38.\]

Consequently,

\[L_1(A^+,B^+)=\frac12\left(\frac38-1\right)^2=\frac{25}{128}>\frac{16}{128}=\frac18,\]

while the new point remains strictly inside:

\[D_1(A^+,B^+)=(A^+)^2-3=\frac38-3=-\frac{21}{8}<0.\]

Thus even a full-batch step can pass the containment test and increase the loss. A stepwise descent guarantee requires additional conditions.

6. Parameterization: what does a learning rate measure?

Question Q1.

The earlier calculations used normalized coordinates so that the relevant scales were visible. Returning to \(\mu=\eta T\) and \((A,B)=\sqrt{\eta C}(a,b)\) explains why a numerical learning rate alone is insufficient to compare two problems. Data scale and target alignment enter the dimensionless update.

Even at a fixed loss and learning rate, equivalent parameter representations can have different local stability. Under \((A,B)\mapsto(cA,B/c)\), with \(c>0\), the prediction remains \(AB\). At a solution, however, the nonzero Hessian eigenvalue becomes

\[S_c=c^2A^2+\frac{B^2}{c^2}.\]

Since \(AB=\mu>0\),

\[S_c-2\mu=\left(cA-\frac{B}{c}\right)^2\ge0.\]

Its minimum is attained when the rescaled factors agree. Thus a function-preserving rescaling changes the normal multiplier \(1-S_c\), and can change whether small parameter perturbations contract. Euclidean gradient descent depends on the representation as well as on the predictor. A coordinate transformation accompanied by a corresponding change of optimization metric would be a different algorithmic comparison.

Scale-aware approaches such as maximal-update parameterization address related questions in wider models. The two-factor example illustrates why scale matters, but it has no width limit and establishes no learning-rate transfer rule across architectures. Depth, normalization, momentum, adaptive second moments, and warmup introduce mechanisms absent from the present state variables.

The practical implication of the normalization is modest and useful: a comparison of learning rates should specify the loss scale, parameter scale, and initialization being held fixed. Otherwise, the same nominal step size can correspond to a different map.

What the comparison establishes

The opening questions and matrix distinguish what each calculation establishes. Their overlap is useful: the same invariant-region identity supports deterministic boundedness, an exact conditional expectation, and a stochastic crossing test. Those conclusions require different additional information.

Three conclusions connect the calculations. First, instability needs a specified meaning. Here the balanced minimizer loses linear attraction at \(\mu=1\), while an invariant bounded region exists for every \(0<\mu<2\). A local threshold cannot, by itself, decide whether a trajectory diverges.

Second, mean behavior and event probabilities require different information. The first two moments determine the expected quadratic boundary coordinate exactly. The example with equal-variance perturbations shows why they do not determine the chance of leaving the ellipse. Neither calculation supersedes the other: they concern different observables of the same stochastic step.

Third, the product does not determine the dynamics. Two factorizations can represent the same prediction and have different curvature and different boundary margins. This dependence on the parameter representation explains why a numerical learning rate alone does not determine stability.

For a larger model, these distinctions suggest a sequence of questions rather than a universal diagnostic. Specify whether the phenomenon is local error growth, sustained oscillation, or a stochastic excursion. Then identify the information needed to study it. The value of analyzing one tractable map is that these choices can be made explicitly, and their consequences checked against the same dynamics.

Derivations

The calculations below establish the map identities, the two-step expansion, and the concentration bound used above.

Derivation: product, scale, and ellipse recurrences

Write \(p=AB\), \(S=A^2+B^2\), and \(\delta=\alpha p-\nu\). The batch update is \(A^+=A-\delta B\), \(B^+=B-\delta A\). Multiplying the two coordinates gives

\[ \begin{aligned} p^+&=(A-\delta B)(B-\delta A)\\ &=AB-\delta A^2-\delta B^2+\delta^2AB\\ &=(1+\delta^2)p-\delta S. \end{aligned} \]

Adding their squares gives

\[ \begin{aligned} S^+&=(A-\delta B)^2+(B-\delta A)^2\\ &=A^2-2\delta AB+\delta^2B^2 +B^2-2\delta AB+\delta^2A^2\\ &=(1+\delta^2)S-4\delta p. \end{aligned} \]

To compute the ellipse coordinate, set \(c=4-\mu^2\) and \(D=D_\mu(A,B)=S-\mu p-c\). Then

\[ \begin{aligned} D_\mu(X^+) &=S^+-\mu p^+-c\\ &=(1+\delta^2)S-4\delta p -\mu\bigl((1+\delta^2)p-\delta S\bigr)-c\\ &=(1+\delta^2)(S-\mu p)+\delta(\mu S-4p)-c. \end{aligned} \]

Since \(e=p-\mu\) and \(S=D+\mu p+c\), the second coefficient satisfies

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu S-4p &=\mu D+\mu^2p+\mu c-4p\\ &=\mu D-cp+\mu c\\ &=\mu D-ce. \end{aligned} \]

Substituting both \(S-\mu p=D+c\) and this expression yields

\[ \begin{aligned} D_\mu(X^+) &=(1+\delta^2)(D+c)+\delta(\mu D-ce)-c\\ &=(1+\delta^2+\mu\delta)D+c(\delta^2-\delta e). \end{aligned} \]

For a full-batch step, \(\delta=e\), so the last term vanishes and

\[ D_\mu(\Phi_\mu(X))=(1+\mu e+e^2)D_\mu(X)=q_\mu(e)D_\mu(X). \]

For \(0<\mu<2\), the multiplier is strictly positive because

\[ q_\mu(e)=\left(e+\frac{\mu}{2}\right)^2+1-\frac{\mu^2}{4}>0. \]

Thus a full-batch step preserves the sign of \(D_\mu\). For a batch step, substitute \(\delta=e-\rho\). The two scalar coefficients become

\[ \begin{aligned} 1+\delta^2+\mu\delta &=1+e^2-2e\rho+\rho^2+\mu e-\mu\rho\\ &=q_\mu(e)-(2e+\mu)\rho+\rho^2,\\ \delta^2-\delta e &=(e^2-2e\rho+\rho^2)-(e^2-e\rho)\\ &=\rho^2-e\rho. \end{aligned} \]

Consequently,

\[ \begin{aligned} D_\mu(X^+) &=\bigl[q_\mu(e)-(2e+\mu)\rho+\rho^2\bigr]D +c(\rho^2-e\rho)\\ &=(D+c)\rho^2-\bigl[(2e+\mu)D+ce\bigr]\rho+q_\mu(e)D\\ &=K\rho^2-J\rho+q_\mu(e)D, \end{aligned} \]

where \(K=D+c=S-\mu p\) and \(J=(2e+\mu)D+(4-\mu^2)e\), as claimed.

Derivation: the two-step expansion and the exact balanced two-cycle

On the balanced line \(A=B\), we have \(S=2p\). A full-batch step has \(\delta=e\), so

\[ p^+=(1+e^2)p-2ep=(1-e)^2p. \]

Using \(p=e+\mu\), the next error is

\[ \begin{aligned} e^+&=(e+\mu)(1-2e+e^2)-\mu\\ &=e+\mu-2e^2-2\mu e+e^3+\mu e^2-\mu\\ &=e^3+(\mu-2)e^2+(1-2\mu)e=:F_\mu(e). \end{aligned} \]

Set \(\mu=1+\varepsilon\), \(a=-1-2\varepsilon\), and \(b=-1+\varepsilon\). Then \(F_\mu(e)=ae+be^2+e^3\). If \(y=F_\mu(e)\), expansion gives

\[ \begin{aligned} y^2&=a^2e^2+2abe^3+e^4\bigl[(b+e)^2+2a\bigr],\\ y^3&=a^3e^3+e^4\bigl[3a^2(b+e)+3ae(b+e)^2+e^2(b+e)^3\bigr]. \end{aligned} \]

Substituting these expressions into \(F_\mu(y)=ay+by^2+y^3\) and collecting equal powers of \(e\) gives

\[ F_\mu(F_\mu(e))-e =(a^2-1)e+ab(1+a)e^2+a(1+2b^2+a^2)e^3+e^4R, \]

with the exact polynomial remainder

\[ R=b\bigl[(b+e)^2+2a\bigr] +3a^2(b+e)+3ae(b+e)^2+e^2(b+e)^3. \]

The three coefficients are

\[ \begin{aligned} a^2-1&=(-1-2\varepsilon)^2-1=4\varepsilon+4\varepsilon^2,\\ ab(1+a)&=(1+\varepsilon-2\varepsilon^2)(-2\varepsilon) =-2\varepsilon-2\varepsilon^2+4\varepsilon^3,\\ 1+2b^2+a^2&=1+2(1-2\varepsilon+\varepsilon^2) +(1+4\varepsilon+4\varepsilon^2) =4+6\varepsilon^2,\\ a(1+2b^2+a^2)&=(-1-2\varepsilon)(4+6\varepsilon^2) =-4-8\varepsilon-6\varepsilon^2-12\varepsilon^3. \end{aligned} \]

For \(0\leq\varepsilon\leq1\) and \(|e|\leq1\), the polynomial \(R\) is bounded by a constant independent of \(e\) and \(\varepsilon\). The terms beyond \(4\varepsilon e-4e^3\) therefore have absolute value at most a constant times \(\varepsilon^2|e|+\varepsilon e^2+|e|^4\): in particular, \(\varepsilon|e|^3\leq\varepsilon e^2\). Hence

\[ F_\mu(F_\mu(e))-e =4\varepsilon e-4e^3 +O\!\left(\varepsilon^2|e|+\varepsilon e^2+|e|^4\right). \]

We can also solve for the positive balanced two-cycle exactly. Write its two states as \((r,r)\) and \((s,s)\), where \(r,s>0\) and \(r\ne s\). They must satisfy

\[ s=r(1+\mu-r^2),\qquad r=s(1+\mu-s^2). \]

Adding these equations and using \(r^3+s^3=(r+s)(r^2-rs+s^2)\) gives

\[ r+s=(r+s)(1+\mu)-(r+s)(r^2-rs+s^2). \]

Division by \(r+s>0\) gives \(r^2-rs+s^2=\mu\). Subtracting the cycle equations instead gives

\[ s-r=(r-s)(1+\mu)-(r-s)(r^2+rs+s^2). \]

Division by \(r-s\ne0\) gives \(r^2+rs+s^2=\mu+2\). Subtracting the first identity from the second, and then adding \(rs\) to the first, yields

\[ 2rs=2,\qquad rs=1,\qquad r^2+s^2=\mu+1. \]

Conversely, if \(r,s>0\) satisfy these last two identities, then \(1+\mu-r^2=s^2\) and \(rs=1\) imply \(r(1+\mu-r^2)=rs^2=s\). Interchanging \(r\) and \(s\) proves the other cycle equation. Such distinct positive values exist for \(\mu>1\): the values \(r^2,s^2\) are the roots of \(z^2-(\mu+1)z+1=0\). Its discriminant is \((\mu+1)^2-4>0\); the roots have positive sum and positive product, so both are positive and distinct.

Let \(e_+=r^2-\mu\) and \(e_-=s^2-\mu\), assigning \(r\) to the larger root. Their sum and product are

\[ \begin{aligned} e_++e_-&=(r^2+s^2)-2\mu=1-\mu=-\varepsilon,\\ e_+e_-&=r^2s^2-\mu(r^2+s^2)+\mu^2\\ &=1-\mu(\mu+1)+\mu^2=1-\mu=-\varepsilon. \end{aligned} \]

Thus both errors solve \(z^2+\varepsilon z-\varepsilon=0\), and the quadratic formula gives

\[ e_\pm=\frac{-\varepsilon\pm\sqrt{\varepsilon^2+4\varepsilon}}{2}. \]

For \(\varepsilon>0\), the square-root term divided by two equals \(\sqrt\varepsilon\sqrt{1+\varepsilon/4}\). The identity

\[ \sqrt{1+\varepsilon/4}-1 =\frac{\varepsilon/4}{\sqrt{1+\varepsilon/4}+1} \]

bounds its difference from one by \(\varepsilon/8\). Therefore

\[ e_\pm=\pm\sqrt\varepsilon-\frac{\varepsilon}{2} +O(\varepsilon^{3/2}), \]

which verifies the leading amplitude predicted by the two-step expansion.

Derivation: a conditional one-step crossing bound

Fix a nonzero interior state \(X\) and its product \(p=AB\). Suppose the batch kick is \(\rho=m^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^m Z_i\), where, conditional on this state, the \(Z_i\) are independent copies of a centered random variable satisfying

\[ \mathbb E[e^{sZ_i}\mid X]\leq \exp\!\left(\frac{s^2\kappa(p)^2}{2}\right) \qquad\text{for every }s\in\mathbb R. \]

Assume first that \(\kappa(p)>0\). Conditional independence gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[e^{s\rho}\mid X] &=\prod_{i=1}^m\mathbb E[e^{sZ_i/m}\mid X]\\ &\leq\prod_{i=1}^m \exp\!\left(\frac{s^2\kappa(p)^2}{2m^2}\right)\\ &=\exp\!\left(\frac{s^2\kappa(p)^2}{2m}\right). \end{aligned} \]

For any threshold \(t>0\) and any \(s>0\), the event \(\rho\geq t\) implies \(e^{s\rho}\geq e^{st}\). Markov's inequality therefore gives

\[ \Pr(\rho\geq t\mid X) \leq e^{-st}\mathbb E[e^{s\rho}\mid X] \leq\exp\!\left(-st+\frac{s^2\kappa(p)^2}{2m}\right). \]

Completing the square in the exponent gives

\[ -st+\frac{s^2\kappa(p)^2}{2m} =\frac{\kappa(p)^2}{2m} \left(s-\frac{mt}{\kappa(p)^2}\right)^2 -\frac{mt^2}{2\kappa(p)^2}. \]

The choice \(s=mt/\kappa(p)^2>0\) therefore proves

\[ \Pr(\rho\geq t\mid X) \leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{mt^2}{2\kappa(p)^2}\right). \]

The moment-generating-function assumption also holds for \(-Z_i\), because it holds for negative as well as positive arguments. Applying the same argument to \(-\rho\) gives

\[ \Pr(\rho\leq-t\mid X) \leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{mt^2}{2\kappa(p)^2}\right). \]

For the safe interval \(\rho_-<0<\rho_+\), reaching or leaving its endpoints is the union of the two disjoint events \(\rho\leq\rho_-\) and \(\rho\geq\rho_+\). Substituting \(t=-\rho_-\) in the lower-tail bound and \(t=\rho_+\) in the upper-tail bound yields

\[ \begin{aligned} \Pr\bigl(D_\mu(X^+)\geq0\mid X\bigr) &=\Pr(\rho\leq\rho_-\mid X)+\Pr(\rho\geq\rho_+\mid X)\\ &\leq \exp\!\left(-\frac{m\rho_-^2}{2\kappa(p)^2}\right) +\exp\!\left(-\frac{m\rho_+^2}{2\kappa(p)^2}\right). \end{aligned} \]

The same upper bound applies to strict exterior crossing, \(D_\mu(X^+)>0\), by replacing the two weak tail inequalities in the event with strict ones. If \(\kappa(p)=0\), the moment bound gives \(\Pr(Z_i\geq t\mid X)\leq e^{-st}\) for every \(s>0\); taking \(s\to\infty\), and applying the same argument to \(-Z_i\), shows \(Z_i=0\) almost surely. Then \(\rho=0\) almost surely and the crossing probability is zero.

This is a bound at the fixed current state. It does not by itself bound the probability of crossing over a run whose state and safe interval change over time.
